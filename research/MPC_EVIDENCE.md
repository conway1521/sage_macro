# Evidence brief 1: marginal propensities to consume, save and earn (FR, DE, IT, annual model)

Compiled 2 October 2026. Every number below carries a verification tag: **[P]** read in the paper text (version stated), **[A]** read in the abstract on the publisher page only, **[S]** secondary, taken from another verified paper's description. Sign convention: MPE is reported as the change in annual earnings per unit of windfall, so a reduction is negative. Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie report the same object with the opposite sign.

## 0. Headline for the model

1. The model's annual MPC of 0.10 to 0.13 is what a one-asset model delivers when few households are hand-to-mouth. Kaplan and Violante (2022, Table 1) obtain an annual MPC of 14.6% with a 2.5% hand-to-mouth share, and 40.8% to 58.7% once the model is calibrated to liquid wealth or to a 14% hand-to-mouth share. The empirical first-year MPC is about 0.4 to 0.5.
2. The model's first-year MPE of -0.10 to -0.17 is five to fifteen times the lottery evidence, which is about -0.01 per year in Sweden and the Netherlands and -0.023 in the United States, and outside the 0 to -0.04 range that Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie (2023) set as the target.
3. The two problems are linked. With separable preferences MPE/MPC = (earnings/consumption) x (Frisch/EIS), which equals about 1 at Frisch 0.5 and EIS 0.5. Raising the MPC to the data would raise the MPE with it unless labour supply is changed.

## 1. Theory and the properties a correct model should display

**Permanent income benchmark.** Under certainty the MPC out of a transitory windfall is independent of its size and approximately equal to the discount rate. Kaplan and Violante (2022, Section 2) give 1 - beta with log utility, which is 0.5% per quarter in their calibration [P]. Carroll, Slacalek and Tokuoka (ECB WP 1655, Section 1) put the annual MPC of representative-agent models at 0.02 to 0.04 [P].

**Buffer-stock saving and concavity.** Prudence (Carroll and Kimball 1996) and occasionally binding liquidity constraints (Deaton 1991, Carroll 1997) lower consumption most at low wealth, so the consumption function is concave in wealth and the MPC is high near the constraint. The summary is in Kaplan and Violante (2022, Introduction) [P]. The three original papers were verified bibliographically only.

**Sources of high MPCs in heterogeneous-agent models** (Kaplan and Violante 2022, Introduction and Tables 1, 3, 5) [P]: poor hand-to-mouth households at the constraint, wealthy hand-to-mouth households holding illiquid assets, precautionary concavity, and ex-ante heterogeneity in discount factors or returns. Their main finding is that the share and type of hand-to-mouth households is the most important factor.

Kaplan and Violante warn that liquid-wealth calibrations abstract from 85% to 98% of wealth and that discount-factor heterogeneity produces a "missing middle" with median wealth 5 to 10 times too small.

**Discount-factor heterogeneity route.** Carroll, Slacalek, Tokuoka and White (2017) obtain an aggregate annual MPC of about 0.2 when the spread of discount factors is fitted to net worth and about 0.4 when fitted to liquid financial and retirement assets (ECB WP 1655, Table 3: 0.23 and 0.43 with Krusell-Smith aggregate shocks, 0.20 and 0.42 with their alternative process, against 0.05 for the Krusell-Smith baseline) [P, working paper version of 2014, three authors]. The published abstract confirms an annual MPC "much larger" than 0.04 [A]. They compute the annual MPC as 1 - (1 - quarterly)^4, which Kaplan and Violante show overstates the true annual figure.

## 3. Country evidence for France, Germany and Italy

**HFCS question.** Variable set `HIZ040x` ("unexpected windfall gain - lottery"): `HIZ040a` is the percentage spent over the next 12 months on goods and services, `HIZ040b` its complement. The derived variable is `DOLOTTGOOD = HIZ040a`. Wording: a lottery win equal to one month of household income, net of tax. It is documented in the 2017 wave (documentation of March 2020, p. 143) and again in the 2021 wave (documentation of July 2023, p. 149) [P, ECB documentation]. I found no entry for it in earlier waves but did not open their documentation.

**Averages, HFCS 2017 wave** (Drescher, Fessler and Lindner 2020, descriptive statistics table) [P, PubMed Central full text, read through an automated page extraction rather than the PDF]:

| Country | Observations | Mean MPC | Concept |
|---|---|---|---|
| France | 13,685 | 0.418 | Share of a one-month windfall spent on goods and services within 12 months, self-reported |
| Germany | 4,940 | 0.513 | same |
| Italy | 7,420 | 0.481 | same |
| All 17 countries | 58,515 | 0.469 | same |
| Range | | 0.329 (Netherlands) to 0.573 (Lithuania) | same, endpoints confirmed in the abstract |

The question was fielded in 17 of the 22 countries of that wave. The authors report that the MPC falls with income and is almost unrelated to net wealth. They note that their German figure exceeds the roughly 0.40 of Djuric and Neugart (2019) [S, not opened].

**Corroboration.** Germany: the Bundesbank Research Brief 35 (November 2020) cites 51% from the 2017 Panel on Household Finances and "almost half" in a May 2020 survey [P, Bundesbank page]. Italy: 0.48 in SHIW 2010 (Jappelli and Pistaferri 2014) [P]. The €80 bonus of 2014 raised monthly spending by about 50 to 60% of the bonus, more for households with low liquid wealth, with wide confidence intervals (Neri, Rondinelli and Scoccianti, ECB WP 2099 and Banca d'Italia Occasional Paper 379, abstract) [P]. Jappelli and Pistaferri (2020) use the 2010 and 2016 SHIW panel and find a strong negative relation between cash-on-hand and the MPC [A]. France: I found no Banque de France or INSEE publication reporting an average MPC out of transitory income. The French entry rests on the HFCS figure alone.

**Asymmetry** (Christelis, Georgarakos, Jappelli, Pistaferri and van Rooij 2019, Dutch survey, Table 1) [P, CSEF WP 467]: nondurable MPC of 0.196 for a one-month income gain and 0.238 for a one-month loss, then 0.143 and 0.240 for three-month changes. Adding durables gives a total of about 0.39 for the one-month gain. The abstract states an average of 15 to 25 percentage points, larger for losses and at low cash-on-hand.

**Comparison with the model.** The model's -0.10 to -0.17 in the first year exceeds the Swedish and Dutch first-year figures by a factor of about ten and the US figure by a factor of four to seven. Two caveats apply. First, lottery prizes are large stocks, where a Swedish prize of SEK 1 million is roughly five years of average earnings, whereas the model's windfall is one month of income. Golosov et al. find larger responses per dollar for small prizes, so the gap may be smaller at the model's scale. Second, the US estimate of a near even split of annuitised unearned income between consumption and lower earnings is close to the static split that gamma/psi = 1 implies. The Swedish estimate of 13% is not. The size of the wealth effect itself is therefore disputed between these two studies. The first-year response per unit of windfall is small in both.

## 5. The Frisch elasticity and the wealth effect

**Micro range.** Chetty, Guren, Manoli and Weber recommend Frisch elasticities of 0.5 on the intensive margin and 0.25 on the extensive margin, and Hicksian elasticities of 0.3 and 0.25 [P, abstract of the companion paper NBER WP 16729, later in the NBER Macroeconomics Annual. The AER Papers and Proceedings article was verified at abstract level only, and its table was not opened]. Keane (2011) reports an average Hicksian elasticity of 0.31 across the studies he surveys [A]. Cesarini et al. obtain a Frisch elasticity of 0.14 from lottery wealth effects, at the low end [P]. A Frisch elasticity of 0.5 is therefore a defensible intensive-margin value.

**Why Frisch 0.5 and gamma = 2 give a large MPE.** Proposition 1 of Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie gives MPE/MPC = (wn/c) x (Frisch/EIS) x (1 - CI). With Frisch = EIS = 0.5 and CI = 0 the ratio is close to 1, so "constrained agents that do not save should have an MPC of 0.5 and an MPE of 0.5" (Section 2) [P]. Their separable HANK model calibrated to a quarterly MPC of 0.25 has an annual MPE of 0.23. Matching an annual MPE of 0.04 with an annual MPC of 0.50 requires CI of at least 0.92.

**Routes offered by the literature.**

1. *Weak wealth effects through preferences.* Greenwood, Hercowitz and Huffman (1988) preferences remove the wealth effect on labour supply (CI = 1, MPE = 0), and Jaimovich and Rebelo (2009) parameterise the strength of the wealth effect between that case and the King, Plosser and Rebelo (1988) balanced-growth class. Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie prove that lowering the wealth effect necessarily raises consumption-labour complementarity, and in a New Keynesian setting this yields impact multipliers above 3 under GHH. Their "GHH-plus" family spans the cases with one parameter, and intermediate values of 0.6 to 0.7 give annual MPEs of 0.040 and 0.028 (Section 4.3) [P]. The original three papers were verified bibliographically, and their content is reported from standard knowledge and from Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie.
2. *Take households off their labour supply curve.* This is the solution Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie recommend: separable preferences, hours set by a union subject to labour demand, sticky wages, so that MPE = 0 by construction (Section 5). Search frictions are the alternative they name, with the warning that an elastic search or intensive margin restores a high MPE (footnote 5) [P].
3. *Parameter changes within separable preferences.* A lower Frisch elasticity (0.25) or a higher EIS (1.0) reduces the MPE relative to the MPC, which they describe as helpful but not a robust solution (Section 4.4 and Conclusion) [P].
4. *Balanced growth.* With additively separable preferences, constant hours along a growth path require log utility in consumption. This is the King, Plosser and Rebelo restriction, stated here from standard knowledge and not read in the paper. At gamma = 2 the model is outside that class, which matters only if trend growth is modelled.

**Implication for a non-monetary Bewley model.** The multiplier objection to GHH is specific to demand-determined output. In a model without nominal rigidities the binding considerations are the MPE target and balanced growth. The literature supports either (i) a preference class with a weak wealth effect, with the complementarity it entails reported openly, or (ii) hours that households do not choose freely in the short run, with the extensive margin carrying part of the response, which accounts for about 40% of the Swedish effect.

## 6. The hand-to-mouth target

Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (2014), Table 5 "Robustness results for fraction poor HtM and wealthy HtM", baseline row, HFCS first wave with 2010 as reference cross-section [P, read in both NBER WP 20073 and the published Brookings version, identical numbers]:

| Country | Poor | Wealthy | Total | Total, weekly pay period | Total, monthly pay period |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| France | 0.032 | 0.173 | 0.205 | 0.108 | 0.402 |
| Germany | 0.074 | 0.248 | 0.322 | 0.219 | 0.456 |
| Italy | 0.083 | 0.155 | 0.238 | 0.222 | 0.279 |
| United States | 0.138 | 0.202 | 0.340 | 0.274 | 0.438 |

Totals are my sums of the two rows. The values in the current calibration are confirmed. Figure 9(a) shows the same shares graphically.

**Which share for a one-asset model.** Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (Section 8) describe the one-asset model calibrated to net worth as generating only poor and non hand-to-mouth households, so that it "greatly understates" the hand-to-mouth share, and the liquid-wealth economy as one in which poor and wealthy hand-to-mouth are lumped together [P]. In Kaplan and Violante (2022) the net-worth model has 2% hand-to-mouth against 14% in the data, and targeting the 14% share raises the quarterly MPC to 22% (Table 1, col. 7) [P]. The reading that follows is: if the single asset is net worth, the poor share is the consistent target and a low MPC is the expected outcome. If the single asset is liquid wealth, the consistent target is the total share (FR 0.205, DE 0.322, IT 0.238), at the cost of abstracting from most wealth. Kaplan and Violante (2014, Econometrica) was verified at abstract level, which states that wealthy hand-to-mouth households drive the high MPC in the two-asset model [A].

### (c) Qualitative tests

T1 to T12 in Section 1. The ones I would make hard assertions are T2 (monotone decline in liquid wealth), T3, T4, T5, T9, T10 (exact adding-up to numerical tolerance) and T8 (present value of the iMPC column equals 1). T11 is a diagnostic: it should hold exactly under flexible separable effort and should fail by design once labour supply is modified.

### Thin evidence and extrapolation

- France has one source, a self-reported survey average. No revealed-preference estimate was found.
- No MPE evidence exists for any of the three countries.
- All revealed-preference annual MPCs come from Norway and measure total expenditure.
- The hand-to-mouth shares date from 2010. Later HFCS waves could update them, which I did not check.
- The split of a windfall between consumption and leisure differs sharply between the Swedish (13%) and US (about half) lottery studies.
