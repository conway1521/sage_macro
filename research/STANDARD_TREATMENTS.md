# Standard treatments: literature verification (v3b)

Status: COMPLETE (2026-10-02). All ten claims checked. Items that could not be opened are listed in the last section.

Verification key used below.
- TEXT (published): read in the typeset journal article.
- TEXT (author version): read in the authors' accepted manuscript or the latest NBER revision, with the abstract matched to the published record. Section, equation and table numbers are those of that version and should be checked against the journal PDF before a page is quoted.
- ABSTRACT: confirmed in the abstract only (publisher page, or the publisher-deposited abstract in Crossref or OpenAlex).
- RECORD: bibliographic record confirmed at the DOI registry only.
- NOT VERIFIED: could not be confirmed.

Every bibliographic record below was confirmed against the Crossref record for the DOI unless stated. Most publisher pages (Wiley, Chicago, Elsevier, Annual Reviews, OUP) refuse automated access, so full texts were read from author-hosted or NBER copies and this is flagged each time. Source wording is paraphrased throughout. Numbers were read directly from the extracted source text, never from an automatic summary.

## Claim 2. One-asset MPC and liquid wealth calibration

**Verdict: confirmed, with one qualification the authors themselves stress.**

Correct statement. In Kaplan and Violante (2022) the baseline one-asset model, with the discount factor set to match mean net worth (4.1 times mean annual earnings, 2019 SCF excluding the top 5 per cent), gives an average quarterly MPC out of 500 dollars of 4.6 per cent (Table 1, column 2). Their first listed finding is that one-asset models calibrated to aggregate wealth give quarterly MPCs between 3 and 5 per cent. Targeting mean liquid wealth (0.56 of mean income) gives 14 per cent, targeting median liquid wealth gives about 33 per cent, and targeting a 14 per cent hand-to-mouth share gives 22 per cent (Table 1, columns 5 to 7, and Section 2.1). Targeting mean net worth including the top 5 per cent lowers the MPC to 2.7 per cent (column 3). The qualification is their second finding: the liquid-wealth calibrations leave out most wealth (85 per cent of wealth in their baseline sample and 98 per cent of total wealth in the mean-liquid-wealth case), so the authors regard them as of limited use in general equilibrium. The empirical benchmark they cite is a quarterly MPC of 15 to 25 per cent out of 500 to 1,000 dollars.

Reference. Kaplan, G. and Violante, G. L. (2022). The Marginal Propensity to Consume in Heterogeneous Agent Models. Annual Review of Economics 14, 747-775. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080217-053444

How verified. Abstract: Crossref. Numbers: TEXT (author version), the final manuscript dated 19 July 2022 on Violante's Princeton page (https://violante.economics.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf5621/files/documents/kv_annualreviews_mpc_v27_GK.pdf).

## Claim 3. Discount-factor heterogeneity

**Verdict: confirmed for the wealth distribution in both papers. The MPC part is shown by Carroll et al. (2017), and only weakly by Krusell and Smith (1998).**

Correct statement. Krusell and Smith (1998) report in their abstract that the benchmark model shows far less dispersion and skewness in wealth than the US data, that an extension with a small amount of heterogeneity in thrift replicates the key features of the wealth data, and that this extension gives aggregate series that depart from permanent income behaviour. The abstract gives no MPC. Carroll, Slacalek, Tokuoka and White (2017) supply the MPC result, and all their MPCs are annual. The certainty-equivalent benchmark and the baseline Krusell and Smith model imply about 0.04 to 0.05. The Krusell and Smith model with heterogeneous discount factors raises this only to about 0.10. Their own model with discount factors spread uniformly around a mean (differing from the mean by about 0.02) gives 0.23 when fitted to net worth, and roughly 0.4 when fitted to liquid financial assets, against an empirical range they put at 0.2 to 0.6 (Introduction and Section 4, Table 3).

A caveat the note should carry. Kaplan and Violante (2022, Section 3.1.1 and Table 3) confirm that discount-factor heterogeneity raises the quarterly MPC from 4.6 to 11.7 per cent (moderate spread) or 18.6 per cent (large spread) with mean wealth held at 4.1. They also show the cost. In the large-spread case median wealth is about a tenth of its data value and 75 per cent of households hold less than 50,000 dollars against 38 per cent in the data, a problem they name the missing middle.

## Claim 4. Labour supply, the trilemma, and the union rule

**Verdict on the trilemma: confirmed. Verdict on the union rule: confirmed. The model's effort rule is a within-state variant of the published rule and differs from it in five ways listed in 4c.**

### 4a. The trilemma

Correct statement. Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie (2023) show that with frictionless labour supply the marginal propensity to earn (MPE) is tied to the MPC. Proposition 1 states, for any individual, MPE/MPC = (earned income/consumption) x (Frisch/EIS) x (1 - CI), where CI is a complementarity index, zero for separable preferences and one for GHH. With Frisch close to EIS and earnings close to consumption this gives MPE roughly equal to (1 - CI) x MPC, so with separable preferences average MPE and average MPC should be about equal. Their facts are an average MPC of about 0.25 quarterly and 0.5 annually, and an average annual MPE of about 0 to 0.04. In their separable flexible-wage HANK calibrated to a quarterly MPC of 0.25 the annual MPE is above 0.2 (0.23 in Section 4.3, with a quarterly MPE of 0.16), at least five times the data. GHH removes the MPE but gives an impact multiplier above 3 and a cumulative multiplier around 5.5. The remedy in Section 5 is to take workers off their labour supply curves through sticky wages and demand-determined labour: households take hours as given, so the MPE is zero by construction. The authors note that search frictions are an alternative route to the same end.

Precision on the wording of the claim. The paper's argument concerns three targets (MPCs, MPEs and fiscal multipliers). The earnings response is too large relative to the data because it is tied to the MPC, which is a sharper statement than "too large relative to the MPC".

### 4b. The union or rationing rule

Published version of the intertemporal Keynesian cross: Auclert, Rognlie and Straub (2024), Journal of Political Economy 132(12), 4068-4121. The year to cite is 2024. The 2018 date in older citations is the working paper.

The cleanest statements are in Auclert, Rognlie and Straub (2024, Section 2.2 and Appendix A.3) and in their review article (2025, Section 2). In words:

1. Allocation rule. The nominal wage is partially rigid, households are off their labour supply curves and take hours as given, and hours are allocated uniformly: every household works the same hours, n_it = N_t. A proportional rule n_it = n(e_it) N_t, with n a fixed function of productivity, is equivalent after relabelling productivity (2024, footnote 7).
2. Who sets hours. Each worker belongs to a union. Each union employs a representative sample of the population, sets one wage per efficiency unit, requires all members to supply the same hours, and chooses the wage to maximise the average utility of its members subject to the labour demand curve and a quadratic utility cost of changing the nominal wage.
3. The average condition (2024, equations A.10 to A.12). The union's first-order condition is a wage Phillips curve: wage inflation today is proportional to the population average of [v'(n_it) minus ((epsilon - 1)/epsilon) x (marginal after-tax income from an extra hour) x u'(c_it)], plus discounted future wage inflation. In aggregates: wage inflation is proportional to N v'(N) minus ((epsilon - 1)/epsilon)(1 - theta) Z u'(C*), where Z is average after-tax income and C* is a virtual aggregate consumption defined by u'(C*) = the average of u'(c_it) weighted by e_it^(1 - theta), that is by each household's share of after-tax earnings.
4. The stationary case. At zero wage inflation the marginal disutility of the common hours equals a marked-down after-tax wage times the earnings-weighted population average of the marginal utility of consumption. With no taxes: v'(N) = ((epsilon - 1)/epsilon) x w x average over all households of e_i u'(c_i). Out of steady state the condition does not hold period by period. The gap moves wage inflation, and hours are set by labour demand.
5. Two variants of the union objective (2025, Section 2 and footnote 8). In the simplest version the union maximises the utility of a household with average consumption, so the condition uses the marginal utility of average consumption, u'(C), and the distribution drops out. If the union maximises average worker welfare, u'(C) is replaced by the average of u'(c_it), which is the version in point 3.
6. A stated weakness and the alternative (2025, footnote 7). Uniform rules create heterogeneity across households in the wedge between the marginal rate of substitution and the wage. A rule that equalises the labour wedge across all households subject to total labour demand removes this, at the cost of high marginal propensities to earn (they attribute this alternative to Guerrieri and Lorenzoni 2017, which I did not check).

Auclert, Bardóczy and Rognlie (2023, Section 5.1, equation 25) state the same rule in one line: aggregate hours are set by a union, and the wage Phillips curve sets N x U^n against w N (1 - tau) ((epsilon_w - 1)/epsilon_w) x U^c, where U^n is the average marginal disutility of labour and U^c is the productivity-weighted average marginal utility of consumption.

### 4c. Does the model's effort rule match?

The model sets effort per state so that w E[u'(c)] = phi E[T^psi] holds on average over households in the state. This has the same form as the average-welfare union condition: wage times an average marginal utility of consumption equals an average marginal disutility of work, an average first-order condition replaces each household's own, and households take effort as given. It differs in five ways, and each should be stated in the note.

1. Scope of the average. The literature averages over the whole population and obtains one hours level for everyone, or hours proportional to a fixed function of productivity. The model averages within each state and lets effort differ across states through state-specific marginal utility. I found no peer-reviewed paper that imposes a separate average optimality condition state by state. This is the model's own variant and should be presented as such. It sits between the uniform rule and the wedge-equalising rule of point 6: the labour wedge is closed on average within each state and remains open across households within a state.
2. Weights. The published average of u'(c) is weighted by productivity (earnings shares). Within a state in which all households share the same wage and productivity an unweighted average is the exact counterpart, so this difference disappears if the state pins down productivity.
3. Markdown. The union condition carries the factor (epsilon - 1)/epsilon from the union's market power. The model's rule has no markdown, which corresponds to the limit of perfectly substitutable labour types and can be absorbed into phi.
4. What the condition determines. In the literature the condition holds exactly only at zero wage inflation. Away from it the condition governs the wage, and hours follow labour demand. In the model the condition sets effort directly. The match is therefore to the steady-state version of the union rule.
5. The earnings response. In the literature the MPE is zero for an individual windfall and also for a transfer to everyone on impact, because hours are demand-determined. In the model the individual response is zero, but a transfer received by every household in a state lowers the state's average marginal utility and so lowers its effort. The claim "no earnings response to a windfall" holds for individual windfalls only and the note should say so.

A difference in purpose also needs a sentence. The cited papers motivate the rule by nominal wage rigidity in a business cycle model. A stationary partial equilibrium model has no wage dynamics, so the citations support the device and do not supply a microfoundation for it in a stationary setting.

## Claim 5. Consumption at job loss, family labour supply, consumption floor

**Verdict: the floor is confirmed. The Blundell, Pistaferri and Saporta-Eksten citation is confirmed for permanent wage shocks and does not cover job loss. The opening sentence is overstated as written.**

Opening sentence. I found no paper that states it in those words. As a model property it holds exactly only for households at the borrowing limit with no liquid wealth, for whom it is a budget identity. For households with little but positive liquid wealth the response is large and below one. Kaplan and Violante (2022) report a quarterly MPC of about 29 per cent for hand-to-mouth households in the baseline (Table 1), note that concavity makes responses to losses larger than to gains (Section 5), and observe in a footnote to that section that large negative shocks leave many poor households with an empty budget set, so that an ad hoc assumption is needed. That footnote is the cleanest published statement of why a floor is required. Suggested wording: households at the borrowing limit must cut consumption one for one with income at job loss.

Blundell, Pistaferri and Saporta-Eksten (2016). What the paper shows (pp. 425 and 432, Table 5): in a life-cycle model of two-earner households estimated on PSID data, a 10 per cent permanent fall in the husband's wage lowers consumption by 3.2 per cent. With a single earner, fixed hours, proportional taxes and no saving, the fall would be one for one. The presence of a second earner with fixed hours reduces the fall in household earnings to 7.1 per cent, taxes and transfers reduce the consumption fall to 6.3 per cent, and the wife's labour supply response (the added worker effect) reduces it to 3.9 per cent. Of the consumption insured through behavioural responses, about 63 per cent comes from family labour supply, 20 per cent from taxes and transfers and 17 per cent from saving. Early in the life cycle, when assets are low, labour supply accounts for essentially all of the insurance (p. 426). Two limits matter for the citation. The shocks are permanent wage shocks, and the sample is stable married couples with continuously employed husbands (p. 389), so unemployment is excluded by construction. For the response to a husband's job loss the paper itself points to Stephens (2002, Journal of Labor Economics 20(3), 504-537, https://doi.org/10.1086/339615), whose record I confirmed and whose content I did not read.

Hubbard, Skinner and Zeldes (1995). What the paper shows (abstract): a life-cycle model with precautionary saving and asset-based means-tested social insurance reproduces the fact that low-income households often hold almost no wealth, because the means test discourages their saving. The floor in words (equation 6): transfers equal the consumption floor plus medical expenses, minus all available resources (assets carried in with interest, plus current earnings), whenever that amount is positive, and zero otherwise. In symbols, TR = max{0, (Cbar + M) - [A(1 + r) + E]}. Transfers therefore fall one for one with assets and with earnings, an implicit tax of 100 per cent on both, and they guarantee consumption of Cbar after medical expenses. Calibration: Cbar is 7,000 dollars (1984 dollars) for households under and over 65, built from AFDC, food stamps and expected housing assistance, or SSI for the elderly, with 1,000 dollars as the low alternative. Unemployment insurance is excluded from the floor because it is not means-tested and is counted in net earnings. Medicaid is excluded because it pays only medical expenses. The interest rate is a fixed after-tax real rate.

## Claim 7. Top wealth

**Verdict: confirmed, with a more exact wording for Benhabib, Bisin and Zhu.**

The shortfall. Krusell and Smith (1998, abstract) state that their benchmark, an Aiyagari economy with aggregate shocks, shows far less dispersion and skewness in wealth than the US data. De Nardi (2004, p. 755) reports that in her model without intergenerational links the richest 1 and 5 per cent hold 7 and 27 per cent of wealth against 29 and 53 per cent in the data. Her benchmark is the life-cycle model of Huggett (1996), which she describes (p. 745) as matching the wealth Gini with too thin an upper tail. Kaplan and Violante (2022, footnote 5) drop the top 5 per cent from their targets on the ground that simple precautionary saving models are not suited to the top tail.

Bequests. De Nardi (2004): voluntary bequests explain the emergence of large estates, accidental bequests alone do not, and inherited earnings ability adds further concentration (abstract). With a bequest motive the shares of the top 1 and 5 per cent rise to 14 and 37 per cent (p. 756), which improves on the benchmark and still falls short of the data.

Entrepreneurship. Cagetti and De Nardi (2006): a model of occupational choice with entrepreneurial entry, exit and investment under borrowing constraints fits the wealth distribution of entrepreneurs and workers, and tighter borrowing constraints give less wealth concentration (abstract).

Returns. Benhabib, Bisin and Zhu (2011): an overlapping-generations economy with finitely lived agents, intergenerational transmission of wealth and both labour income and capital income risk. The stationary wealth distribution has a Pareto right tail and it is capital income risk, and not labour income risk, that drives the tail. "Return heterogeneity" should therefore read "idiosyncratic capital income (rate of return) risk". Kaplan and Violante (2022, Section 3.1.2) treat fixed heterogeneity in returns separately and find effects similar to discount-factor heterogeneity.

## Claim 8. Identification of endogenous social effects

**Verdict: Manski confirmed. The variance strategy belongs to the 1996 paper, not the 2003 one.**

Manski (1993). ABSTRACT: the reflection problem arises when a researcher tries to infer whether the average behaviour of a group influences the behaviour of its members. Inference is impossible without prior information on the composition of reference groups, and even then it is difficult or impossible when the variables defining groups and those affecting outcomes are functionally dependent or statistically independent.

Which paper introduces the variance of outcomes across groups. Glaeser, Sacerdote and Scheinkman (1996), "Crime and Social Interactions". Logic in two sentences: if individuals decide independently, the variance of a group's average outcome shrinks in proportion to group size, so the cross-group variance, scaled by group size and net of what local characteristics explain, has a known benchmark. Positive covariance between neighbours' decisions is the only source of variance above that benchmark, so the ratio of observed to benchmark variance measures the strength of social interactions (in their lattice model it identifies the share of agents who are not influenced by neighbours, which serves as their index). The authors concede (p. 509) that the result depends on how much cross-city variance is assigned to unobserved city characteristics.

Glaeser, Sacerdote and Scheinkman (2003), "The Social Multiplier", uses a different strategy: the ratio of the coefficient from a regression on group-level aggregates to the coefficient from the individual-level regression, which approaches 1/(1 - x) in large groups when an individual's outcome rises by x per unit of the neighbours' average. The paper endorses Manski's pessimism about identification without randomisation. This is the working paper wording and the published version is nine pages, so check before quoting.

Corroboration. Glaeser and Scheinkman's survey "Non-Market Interactions" (read as NBER Working Paper 8053, later a chapter in Advances in Economics and Econometrics, Cambridge University Press) describes the variance of group averages as the simplest method, credits the 1996 paper, and notes its weakness: it cannot control for individual characteristics. The formal econometric treatment is Graham (2008, Econometrica 76(3), 643-660, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2008.00850.x), record confirmed and content not read.

## Claim 9. Moments that pin down the illiquid-asset adjustment cost

**Verdict: confirmed in both papers. The two papers use different cost functions and different targets.**

Kaplan, Moll and Violante (2018). Cost function (equation 14, p. 709): chi(d, a) = chi_0 |d| + chi_1 |d/a|^chi_2 a, a linear component plus a convex component scaled by illiquid assets. Calibration (pp. 720-721, Table 5 on p. 722): with the borrowing limit set outside the model at one times quarterly average labour income, five parameters (the discount rate rho, the borrowing wedge kappa, and chi_0, chi_1, chi_2) are chosen to match five moments of the household wealth distribution, listed by the authors as:
1. mean illiquid assets (2.92 times annual output in the data, 2.92 in the model),
2. mean liquid assets (0.26 in the data, 0.23 in the model),
3. the share of poor hand-to-mouth households, b = 0 and a = 0 (0.10 and 0.10),
4. the share of wealthy hand-to-mouth households, b = 0 and a > 0 (0.20 and 0.19),
5. the share of households with negative net liquid assets (0.15 and 0.15), which the authors say identifies the borrowing wedge.
The moments are matched jointly and the paper does not assign particular moments to particular cost parameters, beyond item 5. Calibrated values: chi_0 = 0.0438, chi_1 = 0.956, chi_2 = 1.402, rho = 5.1 per cent a year, kappa = 6.0 per cent a year. Wealth data are from the 2004 SCF and the hand-to-mouth shares from Kaplan, Violante and Weidner (2014).

Kaplan and Violante (2014). The cost is a single fixed cost kappa paid whenever the illiquid account is accessed. Because of the lack of direct evidence on transaction costs, kappa is set to match the proportion of hand-to-mouth households in the data: kappa = 1,000 dollars gives roughly one third of households hand-to-mouth (poor and wealthy together), the upper end of their SCF estimates, and equals about 0.9 per cent of the illiquid stock for adjusting households. The other internally calibrated parameters have their own targets: the discount factor matches median illiquid wealth relative to average income (recalibrated for each kappa), and the borrowing rate matches the fraction of borrowers. Asset returns are measured from the data.

## Claim 10. Taking the interest rate as given

**Verdict: confirmed that a fixed interest rate is standard practice. The best-verified support is for partial equilibrium as such. The small open economy examples are confirmed at abstract level only.**

Partial equilibrium with a fixed rate, read in the text.
- Kaplan and Violante (2022): the interest rate is fixed at 1 per cent a year and the authors defend the choice (Section 2.1 and footnote 4). Their argument is that policy questions separate into a partial equilibrium household response and general equilibrium amplification, and that fixing the rate lets it move independently of the discount factor. This is the closest published precedent for a methods note on a household block.
- Hubbard, Skinner and Zeldes (1995): a fixed after-tax real interest rate in a life-cycle model with social insurance.
- Kaplan and Violante (2014): returns on both assets taken from the data.

Small open economy examples (ABSTRACT only).
- de Ferra, S., Mitman, K. and Romei, F. (2020). Household heterogeneity and the transmission of foreign shocks. Journal of International Economics 124, 103303. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jinteco.2020.103303. A heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian small open economy hit by a sudden stop.
- Guo, X., Ottonello, P. and Perez, D. J. (2023). Monetary Policy and Redistribution in Open Economies. Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics 1(1), 191-241. https://doi.org/10.1086/723410. An open-economy HANK model. Record at Crossref, abstract read on the NBER page of the working paper version (https://www.nber.org/papers/w28213), which lists the journal article as the published version.
Neither abstract says in so many words that the interest rate is taken as given. These are business cycle models with nominal rigidities in which the domestic rate can deviate from the world rate, so they support "small open economy heterogeneous-agent models are an established class" and not the narrower statement that the domestic rate is fixed.

Currency union member. Bayer, Kriwoluzky, Müller and Seyrich (2024), "A HANK2 model of monetary unions", Journal of Monetary Economics 147, 103579, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmoneco.2024.103579. RECORD only, content not read. It is a two-country union model, so the union rate is endogenous there, and it does not support a fixed rate for a single member.

A closer structural match, for information. Low, Meghir and Pistaferri (2010), "Wage Risk and Employment Risk over the Life Cycle", American Economic Review 100(4), 1432-1467, https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.100.4.1432. ABSTRACT: a life-cycle model of consumption, labour supply and job mobility with job destruction risk, in which food stamps and unemployment insurance are valued as insurance. I did not confirm in the text how the interest rate is treated.

Recommendation. Anchor the fixed rate on Kaplan and Violante (2022) for the partial equilibrium argument, and add one sentence that a fixed world rate is the small open economy reading of the same assumption, citing de Ferra, Mitman and Romei (2020) as an example of the class.
