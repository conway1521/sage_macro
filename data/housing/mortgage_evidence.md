### Country notes on LTV

**France.** There is no LTV cap. French housing loans are mostly secured by a guarantee from a specialised institution rather than a mortgage, and banks lend on income, which is why the HCSF regulates DSTI and maturity instead. Loans often finance notary and other fees, so LTV-O above 100% is common: in 2019, 15% of new loans exceeded 100% and a further 29.1% lay between 95% and 100%, which places the 90th percentile above 100%. ESRB (2019) also notes that home equity withdrawal is not possible in France, which matters if the model lets owners borrow against an existing house rather than only at purchase.

**Germany.** No binding cap is in force and the data on new loans were poor before the 2023 FinStabDEV collection. The only official level I found is the survey mean of 84% in 2018 (Bundesbank FSR 2019). I found no published high percentile for new loans. The HFCS 90th percentile of the current LTV (84% in 2017) is a lower bound on the origination 90th percentile because German loans amortise quickly (initial repayment rate about 3.2% a year, same Bundesbank page).

**Italy.** Italy is the only one of the three with a formal cap, 80% for credito fondiario, which can rise to 100% with additional guarantees. About a fifth of new mortgages exceed 80%, so the cap binds for most borrowers but not all. The average LTV-O is about 70%.

## 2. Mortgage spread over a safe short rate

All numbers are my own averages of monthly ECB series downloaded from the ECB Data Portal API (`https://data-api.ecb.europa.eu/service/data/<FLOW>/<KEY>?format=csvdata`) on 27 October 2026. Spreads are in percentage points per year. Because both rates are nominal and deflated by the same inflation rate, the spread is identical in real and nominal terms. For a quarterly model, divide by four.

Supporting comparisons, 2003 to 2019 means: the average mortgage rate level was 3.41% (DE), 3.29% (FR) and 3.41% (IT), against a mean 3-month Euribor of 1.28%. The spread of variable-rate mortgages over 3M Euribor was 2.21 (DE), 1.81 (FR) and 1.82 (IT). The APRC exceeded the agreed rate by 0.06 (DE), 0.70 (FR) and 0.24 (IT) percentage points, so the AAR-based spreads understate the full borrowing cost in France most. The mortgage rate minus the 10-year government yield was 1.21 (DE), 0.73 (FR) and minus 0.22 (IT), the Italian figure being distorted by the sovereign risk premium in the benchmark.

### Choice of period and benchmark

I use January 2003 to December 2019. The harmonised MIR statistics start in January 2003, and the 2000 to 2002 observations carry status flag "E" (estimated back data). I stop in 2019 because the policy-rate rise of 2022 to 2023 passed through to mortgage rates with a lag, which compressed and even inverted the spread: annual averages over 3M Euribor were 0.57 (DE), minus 0.42 (FR) and 0.73 (IT) in 2023, and 0.23, minus 0.18 and minus 0.03 in 2024. Including those years would lower the long-run mean by roughly 0.2 to 0.35 points. The 2020 to 2021 years are harmless (2003 to 2021 gives 2.09, 1.97 and 2.10) but add nothing.

I use 3-month Euribor as the safe short rate because it is the closest observed counterpart to a one-quarter liquid asset return, and it is common to the three countries, so cross-country differences in the spread reflect mortgage pricing only. EONIA gives spreads about 0.22 points higher. The household overnight deposit rate gives much larger gaps, especially in France, where overnight deposits are close to unremunerated (mean 0.09% over 2003 to 2019). That benchmark is relevant only if the model's liquid asset is calibrated to bank deposits rather than to a money-market return.

One caveat for interpretation: most new mortgages in France and Germany carry long fixed rates, so part of the spread over a 3-month rate is a term premium rather than a credit or intermediation wedge. In a model with a one-period liquid asset and no term structure, that is the relevant wedge anyway, since it is what an indebted owner pays above what a saver earns.

### Country notes on the spread

**France.** The spread was low before 2008 (1.13) and high in the 2010s (2.41), when long fixed mortgage rates fell more slowly than money-market rates. France has the largest fee wedge (APRC minus AAR of 0.70 points).

**Germany.** The most stable spread of the three across sub-periods (1.71 before 2008, 2.29 in the 2010s). Fees add little.

**Italy.** Similar mean to Germany. A larger share of variable-rate lending historically means the 3M Euribor benchmark fits Italian pricing better than for the other two.

## Files and reproducibility
