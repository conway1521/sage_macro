# Benefit level and potential benefit duration: measured European evidence

Purpose: choose a measured European target for the search-cost curvature gamma in the SAGE Bewley model (FR, DE, IT). Compiled 26 October 2026 from the papers themselves where possible. Status codes in the table:

- **PDF**: number read from the full text. Where the text was a working-paper or author version rather than the journal version, this is stated, and published table numbers may differ.
- **Abs**: number from the official abstract or an official summary only.
- **2nd**: number taken from another source (mostly the Cohen and Ganong meta-analysis appendix, Tables D-1 and D-2), not checked in the original. Treat as unverified.

Units: "dD/dP" is extra time in nonemployment (or unemployment) per extra unit of potential benefit duration (PBD), both in the same time unit, so it is unit-free. "Elasticity" is the percentage change in duration per percentage change in the benefit level or in PBD. "My calc" marks arithmetic I did on the paper's own numbers; the authors did not report it.

## 2. Country notes

### France

PBD is measured by two credible quasi-experiments on Pôle emploi registers. Le Barbanchon (2016) is a sharp RD on low-attachment claimants under 50 in 2000 to 2002; an 8-month extension adds about 1.5 months of nonemployment and 2.5 months of registered unemployment, so dD/dP is about 0.19 for nonemployment and 0.31 for registered unemployment. The design is clean and the sample covers about 28% of claimants, but the population is younger and less attached than the average claimant, and the estimate falls with higher-order polynomials (Appendix Table 8). Le Barbanchon, Rathelot and Roulet (2019) give an elasticity of benefit duration (not nonemployment) to PBD of about 0.31 from the 2009 reform, which is a claimed-duration concept and therefore larger than a nonemployment elasticity would be.

The benefit level is now also measured, through the official evaluation of the 2019 to 2021 reform (Dares committee report, April 2025). Two strands matter. The SJR strand gives a net elasticity of nonemployment duration to the SJR of 0.14 to 0.5 across three teams, which rises to 0.4 to 0.7 if the whole response is attributed to the 18% fall in the daily benefit. Because the same reform lengthened PBD, which pushes durations up, I would read 0.4 to 0.7 as a lower bound on the pure level elasticity for that population, under the report's own decomposition (Annex 1). However, the population is young workers with fragmented careers, the period is the post-Covid recovery, and the report gives no standard errors. The degressivity strand gives about 0.25 for the top 3% of claimants (former pay above about 4,900 euro gross a month), from a threshold difference-in-differences. Both are official administration studies, not peer-reviewed, and the underlying Dares and Unédic documents were not read here.

### Germany

PBD is the best-measured estimand in Europe. Schmieder, von Wachter and Bender (2012) run sharp age RDs on IAB administrative data over 1987 to 1999 with very large samples, and find dD/dP of 0.10 to 0.13 for nonemployment and elasticities of 0.12 to 0.13, stable across three cutoffs and bandwidths. Lichter and Schiprowski (2021) confirm about 0.1 to 0.12 for the 2008 extension at ages 50 to 54. All of these are prime-age to older workers (42 to 54) with long work histories, and the German system has means-tested assistance after exhaustion, which damps the PBD response.

I found no credible quasi-experimental estimate of the German benefit-level elasticity. Hunt (1995) uses survey data and is imprecise (CG record -3.32 with SE 2.25), Fitzenberger and Wilke (2010) and Wolff (2003) are structural hazard studies (seen only in search snippets), and the Hartz IV papers I found are either structural or personal working papers outside the admissible series. The 67% versus 60% child rate and the contribution ceiling would allow a kink design, but I found no paper exploiting them.

### Italy

Italian evidence is newer and thinner, and all on INPS records. For PBD, three estimates agree: Scrutinio (2019) finds 0.36 weeks of nonemployment per week of PBD at the age-50 RD (2009 to 2012 layoffs), D'Ambrosio and Scrutinio (2022) find 0.30 with a wider donut, and Sovera (2026, peer reviewed) finds about 0.33 to 0.34 from the 2015 NASpI switch, although that reform bundles eligibility and profile changes. Citino, Russ and Scrutinio (2026, EJ) show layoff-timing manipulation at age 50, which is a threat to the first two. For the level, the only credible estimate is D'Ambrosio and Scrutinio (2022), a cap-based RD giving an elasticity of about 0.30 relative to total potential benefit (about 0.23 relative to the monthly amount), for workers under 50 near the 70th wage percentile in 2012. Rosolia and Sestito (2012) find no detectable level effect from the 2001 reform, but their design is too imprecise to bound it. The two strongest Italian duration estimates are working papers (WorkINPS, CEP).

## 3. Assessment

**Which estimand is best measured.** The PBD effect is measured for all three countries by regression discontinuities on administrative data: Germany very precisely, France and Italy credibly. The level elasticity is measured for Italy (one working-paper RD) and France (official reform evaluations without standard errors, for two special populations), and not at all credibly for Germany. On the user's rule that targets must be measured, the PBD effect is therefore the only estimand available for all three countries, and the one I would use to fit gamma. The level elasticity is better kept as an out-of-sample check where it exists (FR, IT).

**Suggested country targets (nonemployment dD/dP, unit-free):**

- Germany: 0.10 to 0.13, central value 0.11 to 0.13 (Schmieder, von Wachter and Bender 2012, Table II), elasticity 0.12 to 0.13.
- France: about 0.19 for nonemployment (Le Barbanchon 2016, conclusion), from Table 7 days about 0.21 (my calc, 50.0/243). Benefit-duration elasticity 0.31 (Le Barbanchon, Rathelot and Roulet 2019) if the model's concept is paid duration.
- Italy: 0.30 to 0.36 (Scrutinio 2019; D'Ambrosio and Scrutinio 2022; Sovera 2026).

**Pooled European figure.** Schmieder and von Wachter (2016) put the European median at dD/dP of 0.13 and an elasticity of 0.37 (0.41 excluding outliers). Cohen and Ganong give a naive mean PBD elasticity of 0.46 and a publication-bias corrected 0.09, and for the level 0.43 naive and 0.21 corrected, which all lie well below the model's current level response. A pooled dD/dP of about 0.13 to 0.2 is defensible for Europe; Italy sits above it.

**Matching concepts.** Three points matter for the fit. First, the measured outcomes are nonemployment durations (time to next job) except where stated, so the model moment should be expected time to re-employment, not paid duration. Second, the PBD response depends on what follows exhaustion (ALG II in Germany, ASS and RSA in France, nothing comparable for many in Italy), so the model's long-term state must be set before gamma is fitted, otherwise gamma absorbs the exhaustion drop. Third, the RD populations are specific: Germany ages 42 to 49 with long histories, Italy ages 46 to 54, France under 50 with short histories. The model's education cells can mimic part of this, but age cannot be matched.

**Comparison with the current model.** If the model's -0.85 (DE), -1.05 (IT) and -1.43 (FR) are hazard elasticities to the benefit level, the implied duration elasticities are roughly the same magnitudes under a constant hazard. These exceed every admissible level estimate for these countries: about 0.30 in Italy, about 0.25 for high French earners and 0.4 to 0.7 (a lower bound) for French short-contract workers. Fitting gamma to the PBD targets will very likely raise gamma and lower the level response, and the French SJR result is the one level estimate that would push back the other way.
